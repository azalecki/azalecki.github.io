## Abstract 

Physical activity and access to the outdoors have always been intimately tied to the
reputation of Middlebury College. The purpose of this study was to examine how important those
behaviors are to feelings of belonging on campus and how closely tied is a students’ relationship
with athleticism and outdoor activity. I surveyed 31 students from Middlebury College about
athletic/outdoorsy culture on campus as well as their personal relationship with athletics and the
college. In this study, I am presenting both quantitative and qualitative responses from those
surveyed. Ultimately, frequency of physical activity alone was not a valuable indicator of strong
feelings of belonging on campus. Rather, the identity with which students align themselves with (as
athletic) was more important. Students that were white or that did not receive financial aid were
more likely to have positive and strong feelings of belonging on campus.

## Introduction
Middlebury College is no stranger to being a host of physical activity for its students. For
example, Middlebury Outdoor Programs periodically offers trips, wilderness first aid courses, and
encourages participation in the various outdoor facilities on and around the campus (e.g., The Snow
Bowl, gear room, climbing wall, Knoll organic farm, etc.). As a matter of fact, this is a point of
pride for the college and a major selling point to prospective students as its advertised-on
Middlebury’s admissions website. It is not a surprise that there exists a prevalent stereotype that
Middlebury students white, affluent and typically athletic or outdoorsy. By spending time on
campus one can see how this identity manifests itself physically through “crunchy” apparel,
informal conversations between peers, and weekend outings.

On a college or university level research suggests that students’ sense of social acceptance is
possibly the most important variable in influencing feelings of belonging (Freeman et. All, 2007).
Considering that athleticism and outdoor culture is the norm on Middlebury’s campus (whether or
not students conform to that) may have an impact on their feelings of social acceptance and
therefore their feelings of belonging. Sports and physical activity are common on college campuses
to promote competition, comradeship, and a school identity. For many students, their position and
identity as an athlete promotes their social acceptance. A study of 21 young Norwegian women
suggests that sport produced beneficial forms of belonging such as acting as a source of social
support and place of refuge (Walseth, 2006). Although, this study focused on immigrant women in
Norway the results of the study can still be referenced when speaking about young adults in the
United States, especially where sports and athletic culture is stronger in institutions of higher
learning. However, a study found that Anglo male young adults with higher levels of education and
income were more likely to participate in outdoor recreation. Particularly, the level of income was
an equal and or better predictor of outdoor recreational activity than education level (Lee, Scott,
Floyd, 2001)

Although there do exist studies on the influence of sports and feelings of belonging as well
as the influences of belonging on college campuses, few studies have specifically cross examined
how athleticism and outdoor activity give meaning to students’ sense of place on a college campus.
This research allows for an opportunity to consider how important athletic fitness and outdoor
activity participation is in having strong feelings of connection to Middlebury.

Specifically, I ask:
- To what extent, do students feel pressured to conform to the athletic stereotype?
- Does being more athletic increase students’ feelings of belonging on campus?

## Methods 

To document the impact that athleticism and outdoor activity has on students’ sense of
place in Middlebury, I surveyed 31 students on Middlebury’s campus during the spring semester of
2022. By focusing my study on Middlebury students, I was able to narrow my scope to a smaller
range age group but also directly focus on a group that usually has the strongest ties to college
campuses. The survey was voluntary and open to all Middlebury students no matter what their class
year or personal levels of physical activity were in hopes of best capturing some variance among the
student body. Ultimately the surveys were distributed using my own social network through mobile
messaging apps like GroupMe and WhatsApp. A link to the survey was distributed in the
Middlebury class of 2024 GroupMe and the Spanish house WhatsApp group. The survey was also
promoted on my Instagram where I have a substantial following of other Middlebury students. The
distribution of the survey also depended on me asking friends and other connections to distribute
information about the survey through word of mouth. This method I had less control over but
allowed the survey to spread through a snowball sampling method.

The survey instrument included several questions that would measure some control factors
such as: gender, class year, ethnicity, hometown, receiving financial aid, employment, etc. The rest
of the survey questions were targeted specifically to capture my variables of interest. To summarize
the outcome variables students were asked to answer how strongly they agree/disagreed with
statements pertaining to feelings of connection to Middlebury. Feelings of belonging and sense of
place cannot always be easily measured numerically so I created a couple of questions that would
get at different ways that feelings of belonging can manifest. Most of my observatory variables that
measure sense of belonging is found in a matrix table on a scale of 5 possible answers from “Strongly Disagree” to “Strongly Agree.” Table 1 goes into more detail about how I measured my
variables and any other relevant notes.

Most of my variables of interest I coded as binary variables since in the survey they were
structured as simple yes or no questions. For the survey questions that had “maybe” or “not sure”
options I coded those as no or 0 because the respondent did not enthusiastically agree to the
statement. Those that chose to respond as “prefer not to say” I excluded those out of the survey
and coded those variables as missing. For the questions that were in a range of “Strongly Agree” to
“Strongly Disagree”

To analyze the data from my survey responses, I ran several T-tests and one-way ANOVAs
(depended on whether the variable was categorical or not) to check for the significance levels of
relationships between measures of student behavior and measures of students’ sense of belonging. I
then followed these tests with logistic regression models only on the pairs that were statistically
significant.

I followed up the surveys with 6 interviews with some of the students who took the survey
to supplement the quantitative data with more qualitative and anecdotal evidence of students’
experiences on campus. Participation in the interviews was voluntary as students surveyed were
asked at the end of the survey to include their contact information if they were interested in being
interviewed. I supplemented two interviews with people that I knew and that I wanted to speak
about this topic. The interview was structured into three sections of questions: the person’s
relationship with physical activity, their perceptions of the student body and campus atmosphere,
and the person’s connection to campus. Interview questions were prepared beforehand but
whether I used them depended on how the interview went and the topics discussed during the
interview. The interview guide is also attached in the appendix. Some of the interview questions
were borrowed from the survey because I was hoping to get more qualitative information from
them which I was not able to get using survey data.

## Results

A little more than half of the respondents (55 %) in the survey identified as female while
29% percent of respondents identified as male, 13% identified as non-binary (1 respondent
preferred not to answer, 3%). A greater majority of the respondents (61 %) were white with a little
variability in ethnicities: Asian (23), Latin X (6), Other/Mixed (6) and Black (3).

Out of the 31 students in our study 45.16% of them reported that they are from an urban
area, 32% were from suburban areas and 23% were from a rural area. The greater majority of students (48 %) reported that they are employed and work less than 10 hours per week. Also, 47%
of the students reported participating in physical activity one to two times per week. Although the
majority of students do not consider themselves athletic (55 %), 52% of respondents did report
that they were opened to activity after being in Middlebury.

After running several T-tests and one-way ANOVAs, the results are displayed in Table 3.
According to Table 3 there was no statistical significance between frequency of activity and any of
the listed outcome variables. The same goes for being on a sports team or if a student agreed that it
was easy to get involved in outdoor activities on campus. Whether or not a student was employed
seemed to not make any statistically significant difference, either.

Students agree that there is pressure on campus to conform to the athleticism on campus, and that
pressure has an affect on students’ feelings of belonging.
Surprisingly, to the question “Do you feel that there exists a certain pressure on campus to
be fit”, no respondents answered, “probably not” or “definitely not”. Instead, 59% of respondents
answered, “Definitely yes”, 39% responded “probably yes ad 6% responded Maybe. Additionally,
out of the 6 students that I interviewed, all conceded to there being a pressure, as well.

“I think it [pressure to be fit] has actually spread into like how you know international students as well like I remember the first few days I was here I was hanging out with other international students and they were talking about like you know the type of workout they would do at the gym you know like how much weight
you put on your bench press or that kind of thing.” – freshman, male, Asian

“Just like the idea of being fit like as a fat person on campus that is a big thing, too. Like not seeing many fat
people on campus does make you feel insecure at times and insignificant because you don’t see other people
like you. So, if I... like all the people I see are like skinny or built or very muscular and so that will also like
send a message to me like oh I’m not that.” – Latinx female sophomore

“I had a friend who I invited to come on a FIRE trip, and she was like no I’m not built for that or something
like that and I think that’s something that not necessarily true but people have this idea of what you need to
look like to be outdoorsy.” – sophomore, female, mixed race

According to Table 3, between personally feeling pressure to be fit and “I identify myself
with Middlebury” the relationship is statistical significance at the 0.05 significance level. I ran a
linear regression model between those two and included “Receive Financial Aid” and “Is white” as
control variables in the model. The predictors in this model explain 37% of the variability in the
outcome variable. For each positive response (Strongly Agree or Somewhat agree) to feelings
pressure, the likelihood that a response will be also positive for agreeing with the statement “I
identify myself with Middlebury” goes down by 0.2.

Frequency of physical activity is not a good indicator of feelings of belonging, but race and income
level are.
Ultimately, it appears that a greater frequency of physical activity is not an indicator that
students may feel stronger feelings of belonging on campus. Rather, the identity with which
students align themselves with is more important. Additionally, more affluent student that did not
receive financial aid were more likely to have a positive relationship with physical activity and
therefore with the college.
Because there was no statistical significance that frequency of activity, sports participation
or getting involved had on the outcome variable, that is grounds to dismiss those variables as
influences. However, between considering yourself athletic and “Middlebury reflects me” there is a
statistical significance at the 0.05 significance level.
There is a notable and consistent statistically significant relationship between receiving
financial aid from Middlebury and the outcome variables listed in Table 3. The level at which the results were statistically significant vary depending on the outcome variable that was being tested.
There is an extremely high significance between receiving financial aid and Middlebury reflects me
with a significance level lower than 0.001. I would reject the null hypothesis that financial aid has
no effect on the outcome variables. A correlation test between “Receive Financial Aid” and
“Consider Athletic” revealed a slightly negative relationship between them. Which means that those
that received financial aid were slightly less likely to consider themselves athletic.
Although not included in the primary table there also exists a significance level between
being comfortable going to the athletics center and sport participation, race and saying it is easy to
get involved.

Discussion
Ultimately, it appears that a greater frequency of physical activity is not an indicator that
that student may feel stronger feelings of belonging on campus. Rather, the identity with which
students align themselves with is more important. Additionally, more affluent student that did no
receive financial aid were more likely to have a positive relationship with physical activity and
therefore with the college. Students that consider themselves athletic are more likely to feel like they
are reflected by Middlebury College. Some students that were interviewed that considered
themselves less athletic than their peers also expressed that a common accessibility issue is a lack of
knowledge in ways to get involved.
“I know we have the gear room, but I like don’t understand how it works.” – Mixed Race Female sophomore
“I think, one, well I would never seek it out. Like that’s just not me but if I were I honestly wouldn’t know
where to start like I don’t know how people sign up for sports here.” – Latin X Female sophomore
Others that I interviewed who were already actively participating in outdoor activities
reflected some of the ideas found in Walseth’s findings. For example, two of the 6 that were
interviewed expressed that the sense of belonging that they got from participating in physical
activity was a sense of refuge for them.
“I do them [outdoor activities] probably 95% of the time alone but sometimes I like to do them with friends
but sometimes friends don't like to. Yeah but I think it's fun. It can be like restorative, too... like meditative
almost if you're going on a run after like a really busy day. Yeah, it just kind of clears your head.” – White
Male Sophomore
Outside of Middlebury’s campus the findings within this study and these broader questions
are important in understanding the role that college campuses play in their students personal and social development. Two of the 6 interviewees that I spoke to, considered this type of behavior and
the colleges reputation in being implicit in the presence of eating disorders on this campus. One
interviewee specifically said:

“I have friends who really have discussed a lot about eating disorder culture here or how that kind of goes
unsaid in that like this idea that like something else like looking around campus I guess like there is not a lot of diversity in like body shape.” – Mixed Race Female Sophomore

This study is also important in considering the type of student that Middlebury advertises to
and accepts onto its campus. Although the findings of this study did not necessarily point to a
significant correlation between athletic activity and feelings of connection, it is clear that a student’s
financial income/resources have a serious impact on students’ feelings of belonging on campus.
Because my sample size mostly relied on a convenience sampling method, I am worried that
there is some bias in my study and that my sample size is not completely representative of the
entire student body. For example, most of the students surveyed were sophomores that, due to the
pandemic, may not have as strong feelings of belonging on campus as say a junior or a senior.
Additionally, because my sample size only consisted of 31 students, I was not able to test multiple
regression models at the same time. Yet, I believe that these results are a good starting point for
further inquiry as this study could pave the way for more research focused on answer questions
like:

What kinds of students are more likely to acclimate faster to campus life?

How does that have an impact on their social relationships or academic success?

How do college campuses perpetuate the concept of outdoor spaces lacking in diversity and
inclusion?
